# Competitor Analysis & 36-Month Moat Check

This chapter is mandatory in every pitch deck regardless of story structure. It is the single highest-leverage section for surviving investor diligence.

## Why this is mandatory

Sophisticated investors run a version of this analysis themselves before any term sheet. If your deck does not preempt their analysis, two things happen:
1. They assume you have not thought about it, which signals weak founder judgment
2. They reach their own conclusion in private, which you cannot rebut

The right move is to surface the analysis, including unflattering parts, with a credible response.

## Insertion position by story structure

Place the competitor chapter at the position that fits the narrative arc:

| Story structure | Competitor chapter position | Why |
|-----------------|---------------------------|-----|
| Problem-Solution-Market | After Market (Slide 6 of ~10) | Investors have just heard the market size; competition contextualizes the share they should believe |
| Why Now | After Solution (Slide 5–6) | Frame as "who else sees this transition" |
| Category Creation | After category definition (Slide 6) | Frame as "why this category was not built earlier" |
| Founder Story | After Why-only-I-can-solve-this (Slide 7) | Frame as "what others have tried and lacked" |

The biotech subflow (IP/Regulatory/Reimbursement) goes one to two slides after the competitor chapter.

## Section structure

The competitor chapter has three subsections, all required:

### 1. Complete competitor list

List every competitor. Hide nothing. The categories:

- **Existing direct competitors** — companies solving the same problem with similar products
- **Existing indirect competitors** — companies solving the same problem differently (e.g., a service replacing a software product)
- **Potential entrants** — large players whose existing assets put them within striking distance of this market
- **Status quo** — what users do today without any of you. Almost always Excel + email + manual processes + internal tools

If the user wants to claim "no direct competitors", redirect them to status quo. Saying "we have no competitors" is one of the loudest red flags an investor can read.

For each competitor, capture: name, type, rough size (ARR if SaaS / market cap if public / funding raised if private), and what makes them different from you.

### 2. Big-incumbent capability assessment

Build a table of large potential entrants. For each, assess:

| Big player | Existing assets | Distance to our market | Resources they could deploy | Signs of intent | Entry barrier they face |

Examples of "signs of intent": acquisitions in adjacent space, public statements, hiring patterns, product roadmap leaks.

Examples of "entry barrier": existing customer base demands different things, regulatory licensing they lack, organizational slowness, cannibalization risk with their existing products.

This table is honest analysis, not propaganda. If a big player could reasonably enter and is showing signs of intent, write that down. The mitigation goes in the next subsection.

### 3. The 36-month moat check

For each of six moat dimensions, assess: "Could a major incumbent (FAANG / Big Pharma / a major platform player) replicate or surpass our position within 36 months?"

The six dimensions:

| Dimension | What it means |
|-----------|--------------|
| **Technical** | Depth of the technology, time to replicate the engineering |
| **Data** | Proprietary datasets, network effects, accumulated customer feedback loops |
| **Distribution** | Customer lock-in, channel partnerships, exclusive contracts |
| **Regulatory** | Patents, regulatory approvals, certifications, FTO-cleared positions |
| **Talent** | Domain experts, founder pedigree in the specific niche |
| **Time** | Iteration head start in this specific space |

For each dimension, score:
- **Y (1.0)** — yes, they could catch up within 36 months. This is the *risk* answer; a weakness for us.
- **Maybe (0.5)** — depends on their decisions and execution
- **N (0)** — no, structural barrier prevents catch-up
- **N/A** — dimension does not apply (e.g., regulatory for a research tool with no FDA scope)

Weighted total = sum of all Y/Maybe scores. N/A is excluded from the sum.

### Verdict rules

- **Weighted total ≥ 3.0** → **Stop. The product itself is poorly defended.** Tell the user the product needs reconsideration before continuing the pitch — different feature focus, different customer segment, or different positioning. Do not paper over with narrative tricks.
- **Weighted total 2.0–2.5** → Disclose in the deck and explain mitigation. The deck should have a slide explicitly addressing each Y / Maybe with a credible response.
- **Weighted total ≤ 1.5** → This is a strength. Lead with it.

Examples:
- 1 Y + 2 Maybe = 1.0 + 1.0 = **2.0** → mitigation required
- 2 Y + 2 Maybe = 2.0 + 1.0 = **3.0** → product reconsideration required
- 0 Y + 1 Maybe + 5 N = 0.5 → strength, lead with it

## How to present this in the deck

The deck does not need to show the raw moat-check table to investors (that level of self-flagellation reads strangely). Instead:

- **List of competitors**: full, no hiding. Investors want to see you have a complete map.
- **Big-incumbent assessment**: present the 1–3 most relevant players (e.g., the obvious ones the investor will ask about), with the entry-barrier framing
- **Defensibility narrative**: a slide answering "what protects this for 36 months" — based on the moat-check verdict but written for an audience, not a self-assessment

The internal version of this analysis (with the weighted score) belongs in your investor data room or as a discussion topic for the meeting itself, not on the public slide.

## What to do if the user resists

If the user dismisses this as "too negative for a pitch", explain:
- Investors run this analysis themselves. They will reach a verdict whether or not you preempt it.
- A deck that shows defensibility analysis without flinching reads as founder maturity. A deck that hides it reads as founder naïveté.
- The 36-month frame is not arbitrary — it matches the typical time between funding rounds. Investors are pricing in survival to the next round.

If the user is uncomfortable with the verdict, the right next step is product redesign — not slide redesign. Hold the line.
